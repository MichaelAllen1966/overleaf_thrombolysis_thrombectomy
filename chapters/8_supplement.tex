\section{Extended methods}\label{sec:supp_methods}

This section gives the full description of the data and methods summarised in the Patients and methods section of the main paper.

\subsection{Data and patient selection}

We used anonymised data from the Sentinel Stroke National Audit Programme (SSNAP), the national stroke registry for England and Wales, for stroke admissions in the six calendar years 2020--2025 inclusive. Data access was managed through the Healthcare Quality Improvement Partnership (HQIP; \url{https://www.hqip.org.uk/}, Data Request HQIP515).

We included patients who:
\begin{itemize}
    \item had an ischaemic stroke;
    \item arrived at an MT performing hospital within 6 hours of known stroke onset;
    \item had an NIHSS score above 5, \textit{or} a recorded LVO; and
    \item were admitted (either directly, or by transfer from an IVT-only unit) to a hospital that performed at least 10 MT procedures.
\end{itemize}
We excluded patients:
\begin{itemize}
    \item without a recorded discharge mRS;
    \item with recorded use of perfusion imaging;
    \item who received IVT 6 hours or more, or MT 12 hours or more, after stroke onset; or
    \item who were recorded as receiving IVT after MT.
\end{itemize}
Of the 74,678 patients in the final data set, 28,884 received IVT alone, 2,983 received MT alone, 5,164 received both IVT and MT, and 37,647 received neither (Figures~\ref{fig:flow_tte}--\ref{fig:flow_cf}). 

\subsection{Treatment groups and outcome}

Patients were grouped by the reperfusion treatment they received (IVT alone, MT alone, or IVT and MT combined) and by its timing, with each treatment classed as early ($\leq$270 min from stroke onset) or late ($>$270 min). The same groups were used in all three methods; descriptive statistics for each group are given in Table~\ref{tab:full_group_stats}.

We defined a good outcome at discharge as a discharge mRS of 0--2, or a discharge mRS no worse than the patient's mRS before the stroke. The second criterion means that patients with disability before their stroke who returned to their previous level of function are counted as having a good outcome. All three methods estimated the effect of treatment in the patients who received it (the average treatment effect on the treated, ATT).

\subsection{Causal inference methods}

We applied three causal inference methods, each resting on different assumptions.

\subsubsection{Target trial emulation by matching}

Target trial emulation applies the design of a randomised trial (eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, start of follow-up and outcome) to observational data, so that the analysis mirrors the trial that would ideally have been run \citesupp{hernan2016}. Matching stands in for randomisation by pairing each treated patient with an untreated patient who has similar measured characteristics, so that, provided there is no important unmeasured confounding, differences in outcome between the matched groups can be attributed to treatment.

For each treatment group, we matched every treated patient to an untreated patient \citesupp{stuart2010matching} on the features found to significantly affect outcome or the probability of receiving IVT or MT: prior disability, age group (5-year bands), stroke severity band (NIHSS 0--4, 5--9, 10--14, \textit{et seq.}), atrial fibrillation (AF) diagnosis, and anticoagulant use for AF. Matching was with replacement, but each untreated patient could serve as a control for at most three treated patients ($k=3$), so that no individual control carried undue weight in the comparison. Treated patients without a match (at most 2.6\% of any treatment group) were excluded to ensure common support between treated and control groups \citesupp{greifer2021matching}. The proportion of patients with a good outcome was then compared between treated patients and their matched controls.

\subsubsection{Explainable S-learner}

A meta-learner is a generic framework to estimate treatment effects by using a machine learning model to predict each patient's outcome, both with and without treatment, adjusting for all known confounding variables. The difference between the two predictions (with and without treatment) is that patient's estimated treatment effect \citesupp{kunzel2019metalearners}. The S-learner (``single'' learner) is a type of meta-learner that uses one machine learning model with treatment as one of the model's input features, so the outcome under the alternative treatment is predicted by changing that feature while holding all other patient characteristics fixed.

For our S-learner, we used Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) \citesupp{chen_xgboost_2016}, a flexible machine learning model created from an ensemble of decision trees (with each new tree reducing the errors of the trees before it), that allows for interactions between features and for non-linear effects. Following feature selection (Section~\ref{sec:supp_featsel}), the model included prior disability, stroke severity, age, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, AF diagnosis, AF anticoagulant use, onset-to-IVT time, onset-to-MT time, stroke team, and ethnicity. Onset-to-treatment times were modelled either as continuous variables or as the early/late groups defined above. Hyperparameters were chosen separately for each version of the model by a cross-validated search (Optuna \citesupp{akiba2019optuna}) that minimised log loss. No class weighting was used, so that predicted probabilities were calibrated. All treatment effects were calculated from out-of-fold predictions using five-fold cross-validation. To identify the characteristics associated with greater benefit, we fitted a secondary XGBoost model to the estimated benefit in treated patients and examined its SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values \citesupp{shap_documentation}. The SHAP value is the contribution of each feature value to the log-odds ratio treatment effect (treated vs untreated) for being discharged with a good outcome.

\subsubsection{Causal forest}

A causal forest is a version of the random forest \citesupp{breiman2001random}, a widely used machine learning method that creates many decision trees that each repeatedly split patients into smaller groups based on their characteristics (with each tree being trained on a random sample of the data, and each split using a random subset of features). The majority vote of these trees (collectively termed a forest) gives the predicted result. In a standard random forest, each split separates patients whose outcomes differ. In a causal forest, each split instead separates patients who differ in how much they benefit from treatment. The treatment effect is then estimated within each resulting group of similar patients \citesupp{wager2018estimation}. Combining the results of many such trees shows how the benefit of treatment changes with patient characteristics. This lets us estimate the effect for individual patients and for subgroups \citesupp{athey2019generalized}.

We used a causal forest estimated within a double machine learning framework \citesupp{chernozhukov2018double,oprescu2019orthogonal} to estimate the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). This is the expected benefit of treatment for patients with a particular set of characteristics at presentation. The method works in two stages. First, we built two separate gradient-boosted (LightGBM \citesupp{ke2017lightgbm}) models from patients’ baseline characteristics, with five-fold cross-fitting: one predicting their outcome and one predicting which of the eight treatment groups they received. Second, a forest of 2,000 honest trees was fitted only to the variation in outcome and treatment that these models could not explain. The average effect in the treated (ATT) for each treatment group was then estimated by augmented inverse-probability weighting (AIPW), with treatment probabilities clipped at 0.01 and 95\% confidence intervals derived from the influence function. This two-model design makes the estimate “doubly robust”: it stays reliable if either model is correct, even if the other is not. The forest used the same patient characteristics as the S-learner as potential modifiers of the treatment effect; the first-stage models also adjusted for stroke team, year of admission and onset-to-scan time. Onset-to-treatment time was grouped as early or late. Patients with missing data for any model feature were excluded (67 patients; 74,611 analysed).

\subsection{Protocol A: target trial emulation by matching}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Matching.} For each strategy separately, each treated patient is exactly matched 1:1 to an untreated patient with the same pre-stroke mRS, NIHSS band (0--4, 5--9, 10--14, \textit{et seq.}), age band (5 years), anticoagulant use for AF and AF diagnosis. Where several untreated patients match, one is chosen at random (fixed seed, 42). Each untreated patient may be used up to three times within a strategy, and in more than one strategy. Treated patients without a match are excluded, and the percentage matched is reported.
\item \textbf{Balance.} Standardised mean differences between matched treated and untreated patients for all baseline characteristics; an absolute value below 0.1 is taken to indicate good balance.
\item \textbf{Primary analysis.} Proportion with a good outcome in each arm with a 95\% confidence interval, and the difference (treated minus untreated) with a 95\% confidence interval and $p$-value from the pooled-variance two-sample $t$-test. The same comparison is made for discharge mRS and length of stay.
\item \textbf{Sensitivity analyses.} Cluster-robust confidence intervals (clustered on the control patient); control reuse limited to once, or up to five times, or unlimited; matching on exact NIHSS; additional matching on congestive heart failure, hypertension and diabetes; additional matching on LVO; early/late cut-off of 240 or 300 minutes; all stroke teams, or teams with at least 20 MT procedures; inclusion of patients with perfusion imaging; outcome definitions mRS 0--2 only, mRS 0--1 or no worse than before the stroke, and mRS 0--3 or no worse than before the stroke; 20 random matching seeds; E-values; and sensemakr (added later).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Protocol B: S-learner}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Features.} Pre-stroke mRS, NIHSS, age, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, anticoagulant use for AF, AF diagnosis, stroke team and ethnicity, plus treatment: either onset-to-IVT and onset-to-MT times (continuous; not given coded as a sentinel value), or the treatment strategy (categorical).
\item \textbf{Model.} XGBoost classifier for good outcome. Hyperparameters selected by an Optuna search (60 trials, tree-structured Parzen estimator, fixed seed), scored by three-fold cross-validated log loss with median pruning, without class weighting. (The original specification, default settings with class weighting, was replaced by the tuned model during the analysis and is reported as a sensitivity analysis.)
\item \textbf{Estimation.} Five-fold stratified cross-validation; each patient's predictions come from the model fitted to the other folds. For each treated patient, the predicted outcome with the treatment received is compared with the prediction with treatment removed, on the probability and log odds scales. Results are summarised by strategy (mean with 95\% confidence interval) and, for continuous times, by one-hour intervals of time to IVT and MT (cells with fewer than 10 patients not shown). The time at which benefit reaches zero is estimated by linear regression of individual benefit on time to treatment, with a delta-method confidence interval.
\item \textbf{Heterogeneity.} An XGBoost regression model of individual benefit, explained with SHAP values.
\item \textbf{Sensitivity analyses.} Overlap (propensity for any reperfusion treatment); original specification; default settings without class weighting; other cross-validation seeds; outcome definitions mRS 0--2 only and mRS 0--3 or no worse than before the stroke; early/late cut-offs of 180, 240 and 300 minutes; without stroke team; NIHSS above 10 only; exclusion of patients with propensity below 0.05 or above 0.95; a fixed, heavily regularised XGBoost model; a logistic regression S-learner; E-values; and sensemakr (added later).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Protocol C: causal forest}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Features.} Effect modifiers ($X$): pre-stroke mRS, NIHSS, age, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, anticoagulant use for AF, AF diagnosis and ethnicity. Confounders ($W$): $X$ plus stroke team, year of admission and onset-to-scan time. Patients with missing data for any feature are excluded.
\item \textbf{Model.} CausalForestDML (EconML) with LightGBM first-stage models for outcome and for the eight treatment groups (400 trees, 31 leaves, learning rate 0.03, subsampling 0.8), five-fold cross-fitting, and a forest of 2,000 honest trees with at least 25 patients per leaf (fixed seed, 42).
\item \textbf{Estimation.} Primary estimate: the average treatment effect in the treated for each strategy compared with no treatment, by augmented inverse-probability weighting with treatment probabilities clipped at 0.01 and 95\% confidence intervals from the influence function. Also reported: the mean of individual (conditional) effects in treated patients, and the average treatment effect in the whole cohort where fewer than 20\% of patients have a probability of the strategy below 0.01. (The first version reported the mean of individual effects with intervals based on their spread; this was replaced during the analysis, together with a correction to the handling of categorical features.)
\item \textbf{Diagnostics and heterogeneity.} First-stage discrimination (ROC AUC); overlap, as the distribution of the probability of receiving neither treatment; mean individual effects by NIHSS, age and pre-stroke mRS bands (groups of at least 50 patients); and comparison with the target trial emulation.
\item \textbf{Sensitivity analyses.} Original (incorrect) categorical feature handling; without stroke team; without onset-to-scan time and year; additional adjustment for LVO; exclusion of patients with a probability of receiving neither treatment below 0.05; smaller first-stage models (200 trees, 15 leaves); minimum leaf size of 50; cross-fitting folds grouped by stroke team; early/late cut-off of 240 or 300 minutes; the three alternative outcome definitions; three other random seeds; E-values; and sensemakr (added later).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unmeasured confounding}

For each method, E-values are calculated for the point estimate and for the confidence limit closest to no effect. The sensemakr analysis (added later) fits, for each strategy, a linear probability model of good outcome on treatment and the method's covariates, and reports robustness values and bounds for confounders one to three times as strong as pre-stroke mRS, NIHSS and age (and onset-to-scan time for method C).

\subsection{Software}

Python 3.12 with pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn, XGBoost, Optuna, LightGBM, EconML, SHAP and Matplotlib (versions are listed at the start of this Supplementary Material). All code is available at \url{https://github.com/samuel-book/samuel-3}.

\subsection{Descriptive statistics}

Table \ref{tab:full_group_stats} summarises the characteristics of patients in each treatment group before matching. For each feature, the table gives the number of patients with data, the mean, standard deviation, minimum, quartiles and maximum. Binary features (congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation (AF) diagnosis and anticoagulant use for AF) are coded 0 or 1, so their means are the proportion of patients with that characteristic.

The treatment groups differed considerably. Untreated patients were older (mean age 78.5 years, compared with 69.5--74.2 years in the treated groups) and had more disability before their stroke (mean pre-stroke mRS 1.87, compared with 0.41--1.00). Patients who received thrombectomy, with or without thrombolysis, had more severe strokes (mean NIHSS 15.1--17.9) than those who received thrombolysis alone (12.0--12.6) or no reperfusion treatment (12.8). AF and anticoagulant use for AF were much more common in patients who received thrombectomy alone (AF 47--57\%; anticoagulant use 39--53\%) than in any group that received thrombolysis (AF 17--24\%; anticoagulant use 3--4\%), consistent with anticoagulation being a contraindication to thrombolysis. Mean onset-to-thrombolysis time was 122--170 minutes in the early groups and 300--304 minutes in the late groups; mean onset-to-thrombectomy time was 207--210 minutes in the early groups and 357--439 minutes in the late groups. These differences show why simple comparisons between treated and untreated patients would be confounded, and why the causal methods described in the main paper were needed.

\section{Feature selection}\label{sec:supp_featsel}

Figures \ref{fig:feature_selection_1} and \ref{fig:feature_selection_2} show feature selection results across a range of outcomes (mRS outcomes at discharge, mRS 0-2 at discharge, survival, length of stay, use of thrombolysis, and use of thrombectomy). Outcomes that were not about treatment selection were modelled for all patients and for untreated patients.

Sequential forward selection begins with no features and, at each step, adds the single feature that most improves model performance (ROC-AUC for binary outcomes and $R^2$ for length of stay). Each plot therefore shows how performance rises as features are added, in the order in which they were selected; features selected early carry the most predictive information, and the point at which the curve flattens indicates where additional features add little. 

These results should not be interpreted as causal strength of these features, and the full feature set combines features with overlapping information (e.g. onset-to-arrival time implies that the time of stroke onset is known). Given these caveats, we can observe the following.

Clinical outcome is predicted well (ROC-AUC 0.8-0.9 for disability and survival outcomes), and was best predicted by the following features:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stroke severity
    \item Prior disability
    \item Age
    \item Stroke team attended
    \item Stroke type (infarction vs. haemorrhage)
    \item Treatment (use of thrombolysis contributing more to model accuracy than use of thrombectomy), including thrombolysis-induced haemorrhage
    \item Process information: arrival by ambulance, onset-to-arrival time, arrival-to-scan time
    \item Presence of atrial fibrillation and/or use of anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation contributes more to prediction of survival than to general disability level
\end{itemize}

Length of stay prediction has an $R^2$ of $\sim$0.28-0.29. Features that help predict length of stay are similar in order of contribution to those that predict clinical outcome.

Use of thrombolysis is predicted well (ROC-AUC $\sim$0.95) from known features. The most important predictive features were:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Onset-to-arrival time
    \item Discharge destination
    \item Stroke severity
    \item Stroke type
    \item Use of anticoagulants
    \item Arrival-to-scan time
    \item Whether onset time is known precisely
    \item Stroke team attended
    \item Prior disability    
\end{itemize}

In those receiving thrombolysis, thrombolysis-induced-haemorrhage is predicted only poorly (ROC-AUC $\sim$0.66). 
    
Use of thrombectomy is predicted well (ROC-AUC $\sim$0.94) from known features. The most important predictive features were:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stroke severity
    \item Prior disability   
    \item Stroke type
    \item Stroke team attended
    \item Onset-to-arrival time
    \item Arrival-to-scan time
    \item Age 
\end{itemize}

\section{SHAP analysis}

Figures \ref{fig:shap_mrs0-2_all} to \ref{fig:shap_thrombectomy_all} show SHAP analysis for the different models. SHAP values quantify how each feature, given its specific value for an individual patient, contributed to that patient's model prediction. Each prediction is composed of two parts: a base value, common to all predictions from that model, and the sum of the SHAP values for every feature in that individual's case. Because the model allows for interactions between features, two patients with identical values for a given feature may still receive different SHAP values for that feature, depending on their other characteristics.

The units of the SHAP values depend on the type of model output. For binary outcomes (discharge mRS 0-2, use of thrombolysis or thrombectomy, and thrombolysis-induced haemorrhage), SHAP values are expressed in log-odds; summing them with the base value yields the final log-odds prediction. For the continuous regression outcome (length of stay), SHAP values are expressed in the same units as the outcome itself (days).

In each figure, the top plot is a SHAP \textit{bee-swarm} plot. Feature values are colour-coded from blue (low) to red (high); for stroke severity, for example, blue points indicate low severity and red points indicate high severity. The position on the x-axis shows the SHAP value for each point, with points further to the left indicating that the feature is pushing the model's prediction lower (though this effect may be offset by other features, since SHAP values are additive). Points further to the right indicate the opposite: the feature is pushing the prediction higher. Features are ordered top-to-bottom by their overall importance - specifically, by the mean absolute SHAP value across all instances in the dataset, with the most influential feature at the top and the least influential at the bottom.

Below the top plot in each figure are two further plots showing the SHAP values for the two categorical features, ethnicity and stroke team. Because these features are categorical rather than continuous, they cannot be colour-coded by value in the same way as the bee-swarm plot above; instead, each category is shown separately. For each of the five ethnicity categories (Asian, Black, Mixed, Other/Not Known, White), the SHAP values for that feature—which will depend on interactions with other features—are shown as a violin plot combined with a box plot. The violin plot shows the full distribution and density of SHAP values within each category, while the overlaid box plot summarises this distribution using the median, interquartile range, and outliers, allowing comparison of both central tendency and spread across ethnicity or stroke team categories. SHAP values for stroke teams are shown as a bar chart, with one bar per stroke team representing that team's average SHAP value across all its patients. Bars are ordered from highest to lowest SHAP value, so that teams associated with the largest positive contribution to the model's prediction appear at one end, and those with the largest negative contribution appear at the other.

Though SHAP adds explainability to the model, care should be taken remembering that SHAP values show the influence of a feature value to the final prediction, but that this relationship is not necessarily causal (just as nicotine-stained fingernails may indicate high risk of lung cancer, but the stained nails do not cause the lung cancer). These models and plots are exploratory work, investigating which features may need to be included in later causal models.

Some observations may be made:

\textbf{Prediction of favourable outcome (mRS 0-2) at discharge} (figure \ref{fig:shap_mrs0-2_all}): Lower disability outcomes were especially associated with lower stroke severity, lower prior disability, and lower age. Ischaemic stroke patients had a lower chance of favourable outcomes, compared to haemorrhagic stroke (if all other feature values were similar). The stroke team attended also significantly affected the odds of being discharged mRS 0-2. Black and Asian patients had somewhat lower odds of being discharged mRS 0-2. Lower time to thrombolysis or thrombectomy improved the odds of being discharged mRS 0-2, but longer times to treatment worsened those odds compared to untreated patients. Figure \ref{fig:shap_mrs0-2_no_treatment} repeated the modelling, but for patients not treated with thrombolysis or thrombectomy, confirming that stroke severity, age, prior disability, stroke team and stroke type were the most important features affecting odds of being discharged mRS 0-2, independent of treatment.

\textbf{Prediction of length of stay} (figure \ref{fig:shap_los_all}): Higher stroke severity was associated with longer lengths of stay, though dying in hospital was associated with shorter lengths of stay, as was being discharged to a community or early-supported discharge team. Stroke team attended had a significant effect on prediction of length of stay. Shorter onset-to-arrival times were associated with lower lengths of stay.

\textbf{Use of thrombolysis} (figure \ref{fig:shap_thrombolysis_all}): Key features associated with higher odds of use of thrombolysis were: shorter onset-to-arrival and arrival-to-scan times, infarction stroke, higher stroke severity, no use of atrial fibrillation anticoagulation, and lower prior disability. Odds of receiving thrombolysis were slightly lower in Black and Mixed ethnicity groups. Stroke team attended had a significant association with odds of receiving thrombolysis.

\textbf{Use of thrombectomy} (figure \ref{fig:shap_thrombectomy_all}): Key features associated with higher odds of use of thrombectomy were: higher stroke severity, infarction stroke, shorter onset-to-arrival and arrival-to-scan times, lower prior disability, and lower age. Odds of receiving thrombectomy were  lower in Black, Asian and Mixed ethnicity groups. Stroke team attended had a significant association with odds of receiving thrombectomy.

\textbf{Thrombolysis-induced haemorrhage, in patients receiving thrombolysis} (figure \ref{fig:shap_thrombolysis_haemorrhage}): Though this model had lower predictive scores, SHAP is included here for completeness. Odds of thrombolysis causing haemorrhage were higher with shorter onset-to-arrival times, higher stroke severity, lower arrival-to-scan times, and lower pre-stroke disability. Stroke team attended had a significant association with odds of thrombolysis-induced haemorrhage.

\section{Treatment effects}

The main paper reports treatment effects for a good outcome at discharge, defined as a discharge mRS of 0--2, or a discharge mRS no worse than the patient's pre-stroke mRS. This section gives further detail of each analysis for that outcome. Treatment groups are as defined in the main paper: thrombolysis and thrombectomy, alone or combined, each given early ($\leq$270 minutes after stroke onset) or late ($>$270 minutes).

\subsection{Target trial emulation}

Figure~\ref{fig:target_trial} shows treatment effects estimated by matching-based target trial emulation. For each treatment group, every treated patient was matched to an untreated patient with similar characteristics (see main Methods), and outcomes were then compared between the treated patients (red) and their matched controls (blue). The difference between each pair of points is the estimated effect of treatment in those who received it.

The figure contains three plots. The left plot shows mean discharge mRS, where a lower value indicates less disability, and the right plot shows length of stay. The middle plot shows the proportion of patients with a good outcome (discharge mRS 0--2, or no worse than before the stroke), where a higher value indicates a better outcome. Because a separate matched control group was constructed for each treatment group, the untreated (blue) values differ between treatment groups, reflecting the different characteristics of the patients who received each treatment.

\subsection{S-learner}

Figure \ref{fig:s_learner_benefit} shows treatment effects estimated by the XGBoost S-learner. For each treated patient, the model predicted the outcome both with the treatment actually received and with no reperfusion treatment; the difference between these two predictions is that patient's estimated treatment effect. Treatment effects are shown as log odds ratios (treated vs.\ untreated) for achieving the outcome, averaged over patients in each treatment group. A value of zero indicates no effect, positive values indicate benefit, and a log odds ratio of about 0.69 corresponds to a doubling of the odds. Treatment type and timing (early vs.\ late, using a 270-minute onset-to-treatment threshold) were coded as a single categorical feature for this model. Figure~\ref{fig:s_learner_benefit_prob} shows the same estimates on the probability scale (absolute change in the probability of a good outcome), which is directly comparable with the target trial emulation and the causal forest.

The XGBoost hyperparameters (tree depth, learning rate, number of trees, minimum child weight, row and column subsampling, L1 and L2 regularisation, and minimum split loss) were selected separately for the continuous-time and grouped-treatment models by an Optuna search \citesupp{akiba2019optuna} using a tree-structured Parzen estimator, scored by three-fold cross-validated log loss, with unpromising trials pruned. No class weighting was used, so that predicted probabilities were calibrated (mean predicted probability 0.42, equal to the observed proportion with a good outcome). Out-of-fold ROC AUC was 0.76 for both models, compared with 0.75 for the original specification (default settings with class weighting). The hyperparameters were tuned on all patients, so the selected settings have seen every patient once during tuning; with about 75,000 patients the resulting optimism is likely to be small.

\subsection{Causal Forest}

Figure \ref{fig:causal_forest_benefit} shows treatment effects estimated by the causal forest. The main estimate is the average treatment effect in the treated (ATT) for each strategy compared with neither treatment, calculated by augmented inverse-probability weighting (AIPW). Each treated patient's outcome is compared with a doubly robust estimate of their outcome without treatment, which combines the cross-fitted first-stage predictions, the forest's individual effect estimates, and untreated patients reweighted by the odds $P(\mathrm{strategy} \mid X, W) / P(\mathrm{neither} \mid X, W)$. Treatment probabilities were clipped at 0.01, and 95\% confidence intervals were derived from the influence function. The ATT is on the absolute probability scale, so it is directly comparable with the differences in proportions from the target trial emulation. Figure \ref{fig:causal_forest_benefit} also shows the simple average of the forest's individual (conditional) effect estimates in each treated group (mean CATE), which was within 2 percentage points of the ATT for every strategy. Treatment type and timing (early vs.\ late, using a 270-minute onset-to-treatment threshold) were coded as a single categorical treatment with eight levels.

The average effect if every patient in the cohort had received a strategy (the average treatment effect, ATE) could be estimated only for early IVT alone (8.8 percentage points, 95\% CI 7.7--9.8). For the other strategies, 54--99\% of the cohort had less than a 1\% predicted probability of receiving them, so a whole-cohort effect would be pure extrapolation. EconML's built-in doubly robust ATT, which divides by unclipped treatment probabilities, gave implausible values for most strategies (for example, $-$8,082 percentage points for late IVT with late MT), because some treated patients had a predicted probability of their own treatment as low as $7 \times 10^{-7}$; the clipped AIPW estimates used here were stable and agreed with the forest's own averages.

\subsubsection{First-stage models and overlap}

The first-stage outcome model predicted a good outcome with an out-of-fold ROC AUC of 0.76, and the treatment model predicted each treatment group with AUCs of 0.84--0.94 (highest for early MT alone, 0.94, and early combined treatment, 0.93). Treatment was therefore highly predictable from baseline characteristics, stroke team and onset-to-scan time, which shows how strong the confounding is. To estimate the effect of a strategy in those who received it, there must be untreated patients with similar characteristics. Figure \ref{fig:cf_overlap} shows, for each treatment group, the estimated probability of receiving neither treatment. Among treated patients the median was 0.12 (early combined treatment) to 0.60 (late IVT alone). In the early combined group, 11.5\% of patients had a probability below 0.05, compared with at most 4.1\% in the other groups, and almost no patients (one patient with late MT alone) were below 0.01. Overlap was therefore adequate for estimating effects in the treated; a sensitivity analysis excluded patients with a probability below 0.05.

\section{Causal forest estimation}

The causal forest was estimated within a double machine learning framework (CausalForestDML in EconML) \citesupp{chernozhukov2018double,oprescu2019orthogonal}, combining three tree-based models. First, two LightGBM models \citesupp{ke2017lightgbm} were fitted with five-fold cross-fitting, so that each patient's predictions came from models trained on other patients: an outcome model, predicting the probability of a good outcome from baseline characteristics without knowledge of the treatment given, and a treatment model, predicting the probability of each of the eight treatment groups. The features were prior disability, stroke severity, age, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, AF anticoagulant use, AF diagnosis and ethnicity, which were also used by the forest as potential modifiers of the treatment effect, together with stroke team, year of admission and onset-to-scan time, which were adjusted for but not used for splitting. Stroke team and ethnicity were handled as categorical features. Second, the residual variation in outcome and treatment remaining after these predictions was passed to a forest of 2,000 honest trees (at least 25 patients per leaf), in which each tree used half its sample to choose splits and the other half to estimate effects. Removing the predictable part of both treatment and outcome adjusts for measured confounding, including confounding by indication, and small errors in either first-stage model have only a second-order effect on the final estimates. Because it combines an outcome model with a treatment-assignment model, the estimator is \textit{doubly robust}: under the usual causal assumptions, the estimated treatment effect remains consistent if \textit{either} the outcome model or the treatment-assignment model is correctly specified. The ATT for each strategy was calculated by AIPW from the cross-fitted predictions and the forest, as described above. The estimates assume that there is no important unmeasured confounding and that comparable untreated patients exist for treated patients (positivity).

\textit{Little effect on the results.} Correcting the categorical features changed most ATTs by 1.5 percentage points or less. Changing the early/late cut-off changed most strategies by 2 percentage points or less.

\textit{Larger effect on the results.} Without onset-to-scan time and year in the adjustment, the early strategies rose by 3--9 percentage points (early IVT alone 13.2, early MT alone 18.6, early combined treatment 24.0, early IVT with late MT 12.4), to within 1.5 percentage points of the target trial emulation, while the late strategies changed little. Whether onset-to-scan time should be adjusted for is a judgement. It may capture confounders, such as how a patient presents or the reasons for delay. It also largely determines whether early treatment is possible, so adjusting for it compares early-treated patients with untreated patients who were also scanned early, many of whom may have had a specific reason not to be treated. The primary estimates (with it) and the sensitivity estimates (without it) can be regarded as plausible lower and upper values. Additionally adjusting for LVO increased the MT estimates (early MT alone 19.9, late MT alone 11.6, early IVT with late MT 11.5, early combined treatment 18.2 percentage points), consistent with untreated LVO patients doing badly. Without stroke team, late MT alone fell from 9.4 to 6.6 percentage points, and other changes were 1.5 percentage points or less, apart from late combined treatment. As in the target trial emulation, the stricter mRS 0--1 outcome roughly halved the effects of MT-based strategies.

\subsection{Unmeasured confounding: E-values}

The E-value is the minimum strength of association, on the risk-ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the treatment and the outcome, beyond the measured covariates, to fully explain away an observed effect \citesupp{vanderweele2017evalue}. A second E-value is given for the confidence limit closest to no effect. Table \ref{tab:evalues} gives E-values for each method. For the target trial emulation, risk ratios are the treated proportion divided by the control proportion. For the S-learner, the odds ratio is the exponential of the mean individual log odds ratio, converted to an approximate risk ratio as the square root of the odds ratio because a good outcome is common. For the causal forest, the ATT was converted to a risk ratio by comparing the observed proportion with a good outcome among treated patients ($p_1$) with their estimated proportion without treatment ($p_1 - \mathrm{ATT}$). These conversions are approximate, and the S-learner confidence intervals do not include model uncertainty, so its E-values for the confidence limit are optimistic.

For the target trial emulation, point-estimate E-values ranged from 1.58 (late IVT alone) to 3.46 (early combined treatment); to move the confidence interval for early combined treatment to include no effect, an unmeasured confounder would need a risk ratio of at least 3.1 with both treatment and good outcome, given that the main prognostic factors (age, severity and pre-stroke disability) are already matched. For late IVT alone, the confidence-limit E-value was 1.29, so quite modest unmeasured confounding could explain that result. For the causal forest, E-values were 1.81--2.45 for the point estimates and 1.59--1.94 for the confidence limits. These E-values are moderate: plausible unmeasured factors, such as clinicians' judgement of frailty or imaging findings, could be of similar strength.

\subsection{Unmeasured confounding: sensemakr}

We also applied the omitted variable bias framework of Cinelli and Hazlett \citesupp{cinelli2020sensemakr}, which describes the strength of an unmeasured confounder by its partial $R^2$: the proportion of the residual variance of treatment, and of outcome, that it explains. The robustness value $RV_q$ ($q=1$) is the minimum partial $R^2$ that a confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to reduce the estimate to zero, and $RV_{q,\alpha}$ is the minimum needed to make the 95\% confidence interval include zero. Benchmark bounds give the adjusted estimate if a confounder were one, two or three times as strongly associated with treatment and with outcome as an observed covariate. The framework applies to linear regression, so for each method we fitted a linear probability model of good outcome on treatment and the method's covariates, separately for each treatment strategy: in the matched sample for the target trial emulation (adjusting for the matching features and comorbidities), and in each treatment group with all untreated patients for the S-learner (adjusting for its features, stroke team and ethnicity) and the causal forest (adjusting for its first-stage confounders, including stroke team, year and onset-to-scan time). These models are linear stand-ins for the S-learner and causal forest, not sensitivity analyses of those models themselves. The formulas of the sensemakr package were implemented directly, because the Python package was not compatible with the version of pandas used; the implementation reproduces the package's published worked example.

The linear models gave estimates close to the target trial emulation and the S-learner (Table \ref{tab:sensemakr_rv}). They were larger than the causal forest ATT for the early strategies (for example, 20.9 against 15.2 percentage points for early combined treatment), because onset-to-scan time adjusted for linearly removed little confounding, unlike the causal forest's non-linear first stage. In the matched samples, robustness values were 6.8--23.8\%: for early combined treatment, an unmeasured confounder would need to explain 23.8\% of the residual variance of both treatment and outcome to remove the effect. In the whole cohort, where each treatment group is compared with all untreated patients and treatment therefore explains little of the variance, robustness values were lower (1.7--10.4\%), and lowest for late MT alone and late combined treatment.

Benchmarking against observed covariates (Table \ref{tab:sensemakr_bounds} and Figures \ref{fig:sensemakr_tte}--\ref{fig:sensemakr_cf}) separated the IVT-only and MT-based strategies. Every effect except that of late MT alone remained positive for an unmeasured confounder three times as strong as age or as onset-to-scan time. The effects of IVT alone also survived a confounder as strong as NIHSS, even three times as strong, because NIHSS is only weakly related to receiving IVT alone. In contrast, a confounder as strong as NIHSS would remove most of the effect of MT-based strategies (early MT alone 1.2, early combined treatment 6.3 percentage points), and one as strong as pre-stroke disability would roughly halve the effects of early MT alone and early combined treatment and remove those of late MT. Stroke severity and pre-stroke disability are the strongest measured determinants of both MT and outcome, and are already adjusted for; an unmeasured confounder would have to be of similar strength beyond them to overturn the MT results. LVO status, imaging findings and frailty are the most plausible candidates. Within the matched samples, benchmark bounds were uninformative, because matching makes pre-stroke mRS, age and NIHSS band almost unrelated to treatment (adjusted estimates changed by at most 1.3 percentage points).

\section{Extended discussion}\label{sec:supp_discussion}

This section gives a fuller comparison of our results with the published evidence, summarised in the Discussion of the main paper. Estimates for each treatment strategy are given in Table~\ref{tab:effects} of the main paper.

\subsection{Background}

Stroke is one of the three leading causes of death and disability worldwide, accounting for 10.7\% of deaths and 5.7\% of disability-adjusted life-years lost \citesupp{feigin_global_2021}. Although age-standardised rates are falling, ageing populations mean that the number of strokes is rising \citesupp{feigin_global_2021}. Stroke cost European healthcare systems \texteuro 27 billion in 2017 \citesupp{luengo-fernandez_economic_2020}, and the number of stroke survivors aged 45 and over in the UK is projected to more than double between 2015 and 2035 \citesupp{king_future_2020}.

Intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) with alteplase was established by randomised trials showing benefit when given within 3 hours of onset \citesupp{ninds1995}, a window later extended to 4.5 hours \citesupp{hacke2008}. In the individual patient data meta-analysis of Emberson et al.\ (6{,}756 patients from nine trials), the odds of an excellent outcome (a modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score of 0--1 at 3--6 months) were increased by 75\% when treatment was given within 3 hours (odds ratio [OR] 1.75, 95\% CI 1.35--2.27) and by 26\% at 3--4.5 hours (OR 1.26, 95\% CI 1.05--1.51), with no significant benefit thereafter \citesupp{emberson_effect_2014}. Because the excess risk of fatal intracranial haemorrhage, about 2 percentage points, does not diminish with delay, the balance of benefit and harm worsens over time. IVT can be given at any acute stroke unit with brain imaging, but it is relatively ineffective at recanalising large, proximal clots.

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) treats large vessel occlusion (LVO) of the anterior circulation, which accounts for a minority of ischaemic strokes but a disproportionate share of death and severe disability. In the HERMES pooled analysis of five trials (1{,}287 patients), MT substantially reduced disability at 90 days (common OR 2.49, 95\% CI 1.76--3.53), with functional independence (mRS 0--2) in 46.0\% of treated patients against 26.5\% of controls \citesupp{goyal2016}. Its benefit also declines with delay: in MR CLEAN the absolute benefit fell by about 6 percentage points for every hour of delay to reperfusion \citesupp{fransen_time_2016}, and across the HERMES trials each hour of delay was associated with worse disability (common OR 0.84, 95\% CI 0.76--0.93), with no statistically significant benefit beyond 7.3 hours from onset \citesupp{saver2016}. Patients selected by advanced imaging can still benefit up to 24 hours after they were last known to be well \citesupp{nogueira2018,albers2018}, but they are a selected subgroup.

\subsection{Time to IVT and time to MT considered together}

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe how the benefit of reperfusion treatment varies across the joint distribution of onset-to-IVT and onset-to-MT times in a national population not selected by advanced imaging. The existing evidence treats the two clocks separately. The alteplase trials relate benefit to time to IVT in populations for whom MT was not available \citesupp{emberson_effect_2014}. The HERMES analyses relate benefit to time to arterial puncture or reperfusion in patients most of whom had already received alteplase, without modelling when the alteplase was given \citesupp{goyal2016,saver2016}. The trials of MT with or without prior IVT randomised IVT at a single moment in patients who were already at an MT centre, so that the interval between IVT and MT was short and varied little \citesupp{kaesmacher2024,majoie2023}. None of these designs can show what happens when the two times vary independently. In routine practice they do: many patients are treated with IVT at one hospital and undergo MT at another, one or several hours later.

Figure~\ref{fig:main_s_learner_matrix} describes this joint dependence, and three patterns emerge. First, for a fixed time to MT, benefit rises the earlier IVT was given. For MT at 240--300 minutes, the estimated log odds ratio was 0.68 without IVT, 0.69 when IVT was given at 240--300 minutes, 0.78 at 180--240 minutes, 0.83 at 120--180 minutes, and 1.06 at 60--120 minutes. IVT therefore adds substantially to MT when given within about two hours of onset (an additional 0.3--0.45 log odds units across MT times of 3--6 hours, equivalent to odds ratios of about 1.35--1.55), adds less at 2--3 hours, and adds little when first given after three hours and followed promptly by MT.

Second, for a fixed time to IVT, benefit falls the later MT follows, and converges on the benefit of IVT alone. For IVT given at 60--120 minutes, the log odds ratio was 1.71 with MT at 60--120 minutes, 1.50 at 120--180 minutes, 1.22 at 180--240 minutes, 1.06 at 240--300 minutes and 0.89 at 300--360 minutes, against 0.84 for IVT alone in the same time band. The same pattern was seen for IVT at 120--180 minutes (1.43 with MT at 120--180 minutes, falling to 0.62 by 360--420 minutes, against 0.64 for IVT alone). In this population the additional value of MT over early IVT was therefore largely lost by six to seven hours after onset. The categorical analysis says the same thing more simply: patients given early IVT and late MT had an estimated benefit (log odds ratio 0.60) no greater than that of early IVT alone (0.64).

Third, when IVT was given later, it appeared to add more when MT was delayed further still. IVT at 180--240 minutes added nothing to MT performed in the same hour (0.90 against 0.92) and little in the following hour (0.78 against 0.68), but was associated with an additional 0.15--0.2 log odds units when MT did not take place until 300--480 minutes, and more when MT was later still. One interpretation is that IVT matters most when the wait for MT is long, because the drug has more time to act; recanalisation before MT occurs in around one fifth of IVT-treated patients referred for MT and is more frequent in those transferred between hospitals than in those treated on one site \citesupp{seners2016,seners2018}. Cell sizes here are small, and we regard this observation as hypothesis-generating.

The benefits of the two treatments were less than additive. The log odds ratio for early combined treatment (1.16) was well below the sum of those for early IVT alone and early MT alone (0.64 and 1.00). This is expected of two treatments that act through the same mechanism, reperfusion of the same tissue, and it means that neither the IVT trials nor the MT trials can be used on their own to predict the value of a combined strategy at a given pair of treatment times.

\subsection{Comparison with randomised trials of IVT}

Our estimates for IVT alone agree closely with the randomised evidence in absolute terms and in their dependence on time. In the individual patient data meta-analysis of Emberson et al., alteplase increased the proportion of patients with an excellent outcome by about 10 percentage points when given within 3 hours and about 5 percentage points at 3--4.5 hours \citesupp{emberson_effect_2014}. The causal forest estimated an absolute gain in good outcome at discharge of 9.9 percentage points for IVT within 4.5 hours and 9.5 percentage points after 4.5 hours; the S-learner gave 12.5 and 8.1, and the matched analysis 12.4 and 6.3 percentage points. Emberson et al.\ estimated that the benefit of alteplase reached zero at 6.3 hours after onset (95\% CI 5.0--13.8) \citesupp{emberson_effect_2014}; our linear model placed this point at 6.8 hours. The agreement is notable given that the two estimates come from different designs, eras and outcome definitions, although ours is extrapolated beyond the six hours within which IVT was given in our data.

Our relative effects were larger than those of the trials. The S-learner odds ratio for early IVT alone was about 1.9, against 1.75 within 3 hours and 1.26 at 3--4.5 hours in the meta-analysis \citesupp{emberson_effect_2014}. Several differences in design may contribute. We restricted the cohort to patients with NIHSS above 5 or a recorded large vessel occlusion, we measured a good outcome at discharge (mRS 0--2, or no worse than before the stroke) and not mRS 0--1 at 3--6 months, and door-to-needle times and stroke unit care have improved since the trials. Residual confounding is also likely to act in this direction: patients who arrive early with a disabling stroke and are not treated with IVT usually have a reason, such as anticoagulation, recent surgery, frailty or a clinical judgement of futility, and not all of these reasons are captured in our covariates.

The rate at which benefit was lost with delay is consistent with earlier registry work. In 58,353 patients treated with alteplase in the Get With The Guidelines-Stroke registry, each 15 minutes of earlier treatment was associated with an odds ratio of 1.04 for independent ambulation at discharge \citesupp{saver2013}, equivalent to about 0.16 log odds units per hour. Our estimate for the decline in treatment effect on a good outcome at discharge was about 0.16 log odds units per hour. The registry analysis could compare only treated patients with one another; by emulating an untreated comparison group we were able to estimate the treatment effect itself at each time, and so to locate the time at which benefit is exhausted. Time dependence of alteplase has also been examined specifically in large vessel occlusion: in the control arms of the HERMES trials, each hour of delay was associated with an odds ratio of 0.82 (95\% CI 0.66--1.03) for functional independence \citesupp{goyal2019}.

We did not examine intracranial haemorrhage. The trials show that alteplase increases the risk of fatal intracranial haemorrhage by about 2 percentage points irrespective of treatment delay \citesupp{emberson_effect_2014,whiteley2016}, which is why the net benefit of late IVT is small. Our outcome, disability at discharge, includes the consequences of haemorrhage that occurred during the admission, so the estimates presented are net of early harm, but we cannot separate the two.

\subsection{Comparison with randomised trials of MT}

The time dependence we estimated for MT is very close to that reported from the trials. In the HERMES time-to-treatment analysis, each hour of delay to reperfusion was associated with a common odds ratio of 0.84 for less disability and an odds ratio of 0.81 for functional independence \citesupp{saver2016}. In MR CLEAN, the absolute treatment effect fell by about 6 percentage points per hour of delay to reperfusion \citesupp{fransen_time_2016}. Our estimate for MT alone, a loss of about 0.15 log odds units per hour, corresponds to an odds ratio of 0.86 per hour. In HERMES the benefit of MT was no longer statistically significant when arterial puncture was later than 7 hours 18 minutes after onset, although the point estimate still favoured treatment at 8 hours (common odds ratio 1.57) \citesupp{saver2016}. Our point estimate of the time at which benefit reached zero, 9.5 hours, is compatible with this, and in Figure~\ref{fig:main_s_learner_matrix} the estimated benefit of MT alone was close to zero in each time band beyond 9 hours.

The size of the benefit requires more careful comparison, because the comparator differs. The HERMES trials compared MT plus standard care with standard care alone in patients with a proven anterior circulation large vessel occlusion, most of whom received alteplase in both arms; functional independence at 90 days was achieved by 46.0\% and 26.5\% \citesupp{goyal2016}. Our treated groups are compared with patients who received no reperfusion treatment. On that basis, early MT alone was associated with an absolute gain of 12--17 percentage points and early combined treatment with a gain of 15--24 percentage points, and the proportions with a good outcome in the early combined group and its controls (46.5\% and 23.0\%) are similar to those in the two arms of HERMES, although our outcome also counts patients discharged no worse than their pre-stroke mRS and was measured at discharge rather than at 90 days. The trial contrast, MT added to IVT, is best approximated in our data by the difference between combined treatment and IVT alone at the same time to IVT. For IVT given at 60--180 minutes, MT within 180 minutes was associated with an additional 0.65--0.9 log odds units (odds ratios of about 1.9--2.4), a little below the HERMES common odds ratio of 2.49 \citesupp{goyal2016} and the 2.79 estimated at 3 hours \citesupp{saver2016}. At later MT times the increment in our data fell faster than in the trials: to about 0.2--0.4 log odds units at 3--5 hours and about 0.05 at 5--6 hours, against a common odds ratio of 1.98 at 6 hours in HERMES \citesupp{saver2016}.

There are several reasons to expect a smaller increment at later times in routine practice than in trials. Our IVT-only group includes patients without a large vessel occlusion and patients whose occlusion recanalised with IVT, so its outcomes are better than those of a trial control arm in which every patient had a persisting occlusion. Conversely, patients who proceed to MT after IVT are by definition those in whom IVT has not yet succeeded. Several of the HERMES trials selected patients using collateral or perfusion imaging, which enriches for slowly progressing infarcts in whom late reperfusion still helps \citesupp{goyal2016,saver2016}. Finally, disability at discharge may understate the benefit of MT, since patients with severe strokes continue to recover over the following months.

The patients who received MT without IVT were a distinct group: 39--53\% were taking an anticoagulant for atrial fibrillation, against 3--4\% of those who received both treatments. Their results therefore describe MT in patients who were ineligible for IVT, and should not be read as a comparison of direct MT with bridging in patients eligible for both. The finding that MT was effective in this group is consistent with HERMES, in which no heterogeneity of treatment effect was found across prespecified subgroups \citesupp{goyal2016}, and with MR CLEAN, in which the effect of treatment did not differ between patients who had and had not received alteplase \citesupp{mulder2016}.

\subsection{Comparison with trials of MT with and without prior IVT}

The randomised evidence closest to our joint analysis comes from the six trials that compared MT alone with IVT followed by MT in patients presenting directly to MT centres: DIRECT-MT, DEVT, SKIP, MR CLEAN-NO IV, SWIFT DIRECT and DIRECT-SAFE \citesupp{yang2020,zi2021,suzuki2021,lecouffe2021,fischer2022,mitchell2022}. Individually these trials were inconsistent. Pooled by the IRIS collaboration (2,313 patients), they did not establish non-inferiority of MT alone: the adjusted common odds ratio for MT alone against combined treatment was 0.89 (95\% CI 0.76--1.04) \citesupp{majoie2023}. The average effect of adding IVT immediately before MT is therefore small.

A subsequent IRIS analysis showed that this average conceals a strong dependence on time \citesupp{kaesmacher2024}. The benefit of IVT plus MT over MT alone fell with time from onset to expected IVT (ratio of adjusted common odds ratios 0.84 per hour): the adjusted common odds ratio was 1.49 at 1 hour, 1.25 at 2 hours and 1.04 at 3 hours, with absolute differences in functional independence of 9, 5 and 1 percentage points. The benefit was no longer statistically significant after 2 hours 20 minutes, and the point estimate crossed the null at 3 hours 14 minutes \citesupp{kaesmacher2024}.

Our observational estimates reproduce this pattern closely. In the IRIS trials MT followed IVT quickly (median onset to arterial puncture was 180 minutes, about half an hour after the median expected time of IVT) \citesupp{kaesmacher2024}, so the relevant cells of Figure~\ref{fig:main_s_learner_matrix} are those in which MT took place in the same hour as IVT or the next. In those cells, the estimated benefit with IVT at 60--180 minutes exceeded that of MT alone at the same time by about 0.1--0.3 log odds units (odds ratios of about 1.1--1.35), and with IVT after 180 minutes the difference was nil or small (0.90 against 0.92 with MT at 180--240 minutes; 0.69 against 0.68 at 240--300 minutes). That two entirely different designs, one randomised and one emulated from registry data, locate the loss of added benefit at about three hours strengthens both findings.

Our data extend the trial evidence in two directions that the trials could not address. First, the IRIS authors caution that their results apply only to patients who present directly to an MT centre \citesupp{kaesmacher2024}. In the UK, IVT at a local unit followed by secondary transfer is the predominant model of MT provision \citesupp{allen2022}, and in our data MT frequently followed IVT by two hours or more. Figure~\ref{fig:main_s_learner_matrix} covers these patients, and suggests that for them early IVT is worth considerably more than the trials of direct presenters imply (an additional 0.3--0.45 log odds units when IVT is given at 60--120 minutes and MT follows at 3--6 hours). Second, the trials varied only whether IVT was given, at one point in time; we describe how benefit changes as each treatment time moves while the other is held fixed.

More recent trials are consistent with our findings. In BRIDGE-TNK, in patients presenting within 4.5 hours, tenecteplase before MT increased functional independence at 90 days from 44.1\% to 52.9\% \citesupp{qiu2025}. In TNK-PLUS, in patients presenting 4.5--24 hours after onset, tenecteplase before MT made no difference (44.2\% against 43.2\%) \citesupp{tnkplus2026}. A secondary analysis of SWIFT DIRECT alone did not find clear evidence that the effect of bridging depended on onset-to-needle time, although it was not powered to do so \citesupp{meinel2022}.

\subsection{Comparison with registry and other observational evidence}

Large registries have confirmed, among treated patients, that the time dependence seen in trials holds in routine care. In the Get With The Guidelines-Stroke registry (6,756 patients, onset to puncture within 8 hours), earlier MT was associated with better outcomes at discharge, and the relationship was non-linear, with a steeper gradient between 30 and 270 minutes than between 271 and 480 minutes \citesupp{jahan2019}. Our continuous estimates for MT alone show the same shape, falling steeply over the first four hours and more slowly thereafter, and it is of interest that the inflection identified in that registry coincides with the 270-minute threshold used to define early and late treatment here. In the MR CLEAN Registry (1,488 patients), each hour of delay to the start of MT was associated with an adjusted common odds ratio of 0.83 for functional outcome \citesupp{mulder2018}, almost identical to our 0.86.

Observational comparisons of MT with and without prior IVT have generally favoured combined treatment. In the MR CLEAN Registry, IVT before MT was associated with better functional outcome (adjusted common odds ratio 1.47) \citesupp{chalos2019}, and in a recent analysis of 7,118 patients from 46 centres it was associated with functional independence whether MT took place within or after 4.5 hours (adjusted odds ratios 1.56 and 1.27), with no significant interaction with the MT time window \citesupp{chacon2026}. These analyses classify patients by whether IVT was given and by when MT took place, but not by when IVT was given. Our results suggest that this third quantity is the important one: the time of MT alone does not determine whether IVT adds benefit, whereas the time of IVT, and the interval that follows it, do. Among patients who received both treatments in a Berlin registry, a shorter interval between IVT and MT was associated with better outcome at three months \citesupp{wagner2022}, which corresponds to our finding that, for a fixed time to IVT, benefit falls as MT is delayed.

These registry studies share a limitation that our design addresses in part. They compare treated patients with other treated patients, so they can estimate how outcome varies with time or with co-treatment, but not the effect of treatment against no treatment at each time. By constructing matched or model-based untreated comparators from the same national registry, we estimated treatment effects directly, at the cost of stronger assumptions about confounding, discussed in the main paper.

\subsection{Late treatment and the late-window trials}

Our finding that the benefit of MT declines to nothing by about nine and a half hours appears to conflict with the trials of late MT, but the populations are different. DAWN and DEFUSE 3 enrolled patients 6--24 hours after they were last known to be well who had a small infarct core relative to their clinical deficit or perfusion lesion \citesupp{nogueira2018,albers2018}. In the AURORA pooled analysis of patients randomised more than six hours after onset, functional independence was achieved by 45.9\% with MT and 19.3\% without (adjusted common odds ratio 2.54), an effect as large as that seen in the early window \citesupp{jovin2022}. MR CLEAN-LATE found benefit at 6--24 hours in patients selected only by the presence of collateral flow on CT angiography \citesupp{olthuis2023}, and observational data suggest that patients selected in the late window by non-contrast CT have outcomes similar to those selected by perfusion imaging \citesupp{nguyen2022}.

Patients in those trials presented late and were chosen because imaging showed that salvageable tissue remained; they are, by selection, patients whose infarcts progress slowly. The patients we studied all arrived within six hours of a known time of onset, and those treated late were, we presume, treated late mainly because of delay, not because imaging had identified them as late-window candidates. Our estimates therefore describe what delay costs the general population of early presenters, in whom fast and slow progressors are mixed. They do not describe the effect of MT in imaging-selected late presenters, and should not be used to argue against late-window treatment. The two bodies of evidence are complementary: the late-window trials show who can still benefit when presentation is late, and our data show how much is lost, on average, when treatment of an early presenter is delayed.

Late MT was nonetheless associated with a clear benefit in our data. MT alone after 270 minutes was associated with an absolute gain in good outcome at discharge of 7.8--9.4 percentage points, and all three methods found this to be significantly greater than zero. The number of patients treated beyond nine hours was small, and estimates in that range are imprecise. The group that received both treatments late contained only 141 patients, and its wide confidence intervals do not allow any conclusion; the randomised evidence for that combination is the TNK-PLUS trial, which found no benefit of IVT before MT at 4.5--24 hours \citesupp{tnkplus2026}.

\subsection{Agreement between methods, and who benefits}

The three causal methods agreed on the pattern of benefit. Matching, the S-learner and the causal forest make different modelling assumptions: matching compares like with like on a small set of prognostic features without a model of outcome, the S-learner relies on a flexible outcome model, and the causal forest combines an outcome model with a model of treatment assignment and is consistent if either is correctly specified. All three agreed that early combined treatment gave the largest benefit, followed by early MT alone, and that later treatment gave less, although the order of the smaller late-treatment groups differed between methods. Where the methods estimate the same quantity, the absolute gains from matching and from the causal forest agreed closely for the late strategies (for example 8.3 and 9.4 percentage points for late MT alone), but the causal forest gave smaller gains for the early strategies: 12.4 and 9.9 percentage points for early IVT alone, 17.2 and 12.1 for early MT alone, and 23.5 and 15.2 for early combined treatment. The sensitivity analyses showed that this difference arises almost entirely from the causal forest's adjustment for onset-to-scan time; without it, the causal forest gave 13.2, 18.6 and 24.0 percentage points, within 1.5 percentage points of matching (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Agreement between methods does not remove bias from confounders that none of them observe, but it makes it unlikely that the findings are an artefact of one model.

The agreement with randomised trials, where trials exist, serves as a further check. The emulation recovered the absolute benefit of IVT and the time at which it is exhausted \citesupp{emberson_effect_2014}, the hourly decay of MT benefit \citesupp{fransen_time_2016,saver2016}, and the time dependence of the added value of IVT before MT \citesupp{kaesmacher2024}. Benchmarking an emulated target trial \citesupp{hernan2016} against known trial results is a recognised means of assessing whether observational data can support causal conclusions, and it gives some confidence in the estimates for which no trial exists, namely those for specific combinations of the two treatment times.

Our estimates are of the effect of treatment in those who received it, and the treated groups differed from one another. Patients who received MT had more severe strokes and less prior disability than those who received IVT alone, and those who received MT without IVT were far more likely to have atrial fibrillation and to be anticoagulated. Differences in estimated benefit between groups therefore reflect differences in the patients as well as in the treatments and their timing. The secondary model of estimated benefit indicated which characteristics matter most. Benefit increased with stroke severity and decreased with pre-stroke disability and in the oldest patients; after stroke severity and the type and timing of treatment, the admitting stroke team was the most influential feature. Variation between teams, after accounting for patient characteristics and treatment times, suggests that how treatment is delivered, including patient selection, procedural success and post-procedural care, influences how much patients gain. It may also reflect differences between teams in case mix that we did not measure and in how discharge disability is assessed, and it deserves separate study.


\subsection{Strengths and limitations in full}

The study uses a national registry covering six years of stroke admissions in England and Wales, giving a cohort around nine times the size of the pooled IVT trials. Patients were not selected by advanced imaging or by trial exclusion criteria, three causal methods with different assumptions were applied within an explicit target trial framework, treatment times were analysed both in categories and continuously, and all code is openly available.

The principal limitation is that treatment was not randomised. The effects of IVT alone would survive unmeasured confounding as strong as any measured characteristic, but the MT effects could be overturned by a hidden factor as strong as stroke severity or pre-stroke disability, beyond those already accounted for; LVO status and imaging findings could plausibly be this strong. The estimates also changed when comorbidities, LVO or onset-to-scan time were added to the adjustment, so the choice of confounders matters. We had no imaging data, so occlusion site, early ischaemic change and collateral status could not be included. An LVO is confirmed in every patient who undergoes MT but not in their comparators, which would lead to underestimation of the benefit of MT (consistent with this, additionally matching or adjusting for LVO, where recorded, increased the estimated benefit of MT); conversely, patients who arrive early with a severe stroke and receive no reperfusion treatment have often been judged unsuitable, which would lead to overestimation. Treatment groups are defined by events after arrival: patients whose occlusion recanalised after IVT did not proceed to MT and are counted in the IVT-only group, which favours IVT alone, and patients must survive to receive a late treatment. Outcome was assessed at discharge, without blinding, rather than at 90 days; discharge mRS predicts 90-day mRS imperfectly \citesupp{elhabr2021}, and continued recovery after MT may make our estimates for MT conservative.

Time of onset was known for all patients, so the findings do not extend to wake-up stroke. The 270-minute threshold is arbitrary, although the continuous analyses do not depend on it. Some combinations of treatment times were rare, the times at which benefit reached zero are extrapolations, and the S-learner confidence intervals do not include model uncertainty. We did not distinguish alteplase from tenecteplase or examine haemorrhage and mortality separately, and the results may not apply to milder strokes, to hospitals performing few MT procedures, or to other health systems. Our findings complement rather than replace randomised trials, and are intended to support the planning and improvement of IVT and MT services in England and Wales.

\subsection{Implications for practice, services and research in full}

The results support giving IVT as early as possible to eligible patients who are also candidates for MT, particularly those who face a wait or a transfer before MT, and give no support to withholding early IVT in order to proceed to MT. They also suggest, in agreement with the trials \citesupp{kaesmacher2024,tnkplus2026}, that IVT first given more than about three hours after onset adds little when MT follows promptly, although this concerns average benefit and is not a basis for individual treatment decisions.

For services, most of the additional value of MT over early IVT was lost when MT was delayed beyond about six hours. With an MT rate of 3.9\% in England in the 2024 national audit, and median door-in-door-out times of two to three hours at referring hospitals \citesupp{nagaratnam2025}, delay not only limits access but removes much of the benefit for those eventually treated. Shortening the interval between IVT and MT, by faster transfer or by bringing MT closer to patients, is likely to yield substantial gains. Conveying patients directly to an MT centre rather than treating first at the nearest unit trades earlier MT against later IVT; in the RACECAT trial, direct transport made no difference to disability \citesupp{perezdelaossa2022}, which our estimates make plausible, since the gain and the loss are of broadly similar size. Estimates of benefit by both treatment times provide the input that models of service configuration require \citesupp{allen2022}.
