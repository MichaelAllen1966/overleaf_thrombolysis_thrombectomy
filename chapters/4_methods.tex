\section{Methods}

\subsection{Code}

All code and results are available at \url{https://github.com/samuel-book/samuel-3}.

\subsection{Data}

Anonymised patient data were retrieved for stroke admissions to hospitals in England and Wales for six calendar years, 2020–2025 inclusive, obtained from the national stroke registry, the Sentinel Stroke National Audit Programme (SSNAP). For this study, data were limited to patients arriving at hospital within 6 hours of known stroke onset, who had an ischaemic stroke, had a stroke severity (NIHSS) of more than 5 (or were registered as having a large vessel occlusion), arrived at a hospital that had at least 10 MT procedures in the data set, and did not receive IVT more than 6 hours, or MT more than 12 hours, after stroke onset. The size of the final data set was 59,570 patients. Of these, 26,923 received IVT (24,047 IVT alone and 2,876 with MT), and 4,609 received MT (1,733 MT alone and 2,876 with IVT). Data access was managed through HQIP (\url{https://www.hqip.org.uk/}, Data Request HQIP515).

\subsection{Treatment groups}

Across all methods, patients were grouped by the reperfusion treatment they received and its timing. Strategies were defined by early ($\leq$270 min from onset) or late ($>$270 min from onset) IVT, MT, or their combination.

Descriptive statistics for each treatment group are given in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Causal Inference Methods}

We applied three alternative causal inference methods to estimate the treatment effect sizes. Here we report how each treatment affected the probability or odds of being discharged with no or slight disability (mRS 0--2); these methods were also applied to all possible discharge mRS thresholds (see Supplementary Material).

\subsubsection{Target Trial Emulation by Matching}

To emulate a clinical trial, we constructed comparable treatment and control groups by finding a matched untreated patient for each patient in each treatment group \cite{stuart2010matching}. Patients were matched on features found to significantly affect outcome or probability of receiving IVT or MT, namely prior disability, age group (5-year bands), stroke severity band (NIHSS 0--4, 5--9, 10--14, \textit{et seq.}), anticoagulant use for atrial fibrillation (AF), and AF diagnosis. Non-matched treated patients (a maximum of 2.3\% of any treatment group) were dropped from the analysis to ensure common support between control and treated groups \cite{greifer2021matching}. 

Each treatment group had its own matched untreated control group. The proportion of patients discharged with mRS 0--2 was then compared between control and treated patients in each treatment group. 

\subsubsection{Explainable S-Learner}

Our S-Learner was a single model that combined treated and untreated patients to predict outcome, including all features that significantly affected outcome or the likelihood of receiving treatment \cite{kunzel2019metalearners}. Model prediction was repeated with counterfactual treatment options to predict outcomes under different treatment strategies. We used Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) \cite{chen_xgboost_2016} as a flexible machine learning model, allowing for interactions between features and for non-linear effects of feature values. Because the XGBoost model was hard to interpret at an individual or global level, we added an explainability model using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) \cite{shap_documentation}. We limited our counterfactual analysis to predicting the effect of treatment only in those patients who actually received each treatment (the average treatment effect on the treated, ATT).

Following feature selection (see Supplementary Material for details), the following features were included in the model: prior disability, stroke severity, age, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, AF anticoagulant use, AF diagnosis, onset to IVT time, onset to MT time, stroke team and ethnicity. Onset to IVT time and onset to MT time were modelled either as continuous variables or according to the early/late groups above.

A secondary model was fitted to the estimated treatment benefit in treated patients, to better understand the relationship between patient and treatment features and the benefit achieved. 

\subsubsection{Causal Forest}

We used an orthogonal causal forest \cite{oprescu2019orthogonal} to estimate conditional average treatment effects—that is, the expected effect of treatment for patients with particular combinations of baseline characteristics. This approach combined three tree-based models. First, flexible LightGBM prediction models \cite{ke2017lightgbm} estimated each patient’s expected outcome and likelihood of receiving treatment from the measured baseline features. The remaining residual variation in treatment and outcome, after accounting for these features, was then passed to a causal forest algorithm. This approach allowed for complex and non-linear relationships among features, and the causal forest partitioned patients so as to capture how treatment effects vary between individuals. This estimator is considered \textit{doubly robust} because it combines two complementary models: an outcome model, which estimates expected outcomes given patients’ baseline characteristics, and a treatment-assignment model, which estimates each patient’s probability of receiving treatment given those characteristics. Under the usual causal assumptions, the estimated treatment effect remains consistent if \textit{either} the outcome model or the treatment-assignment model is correctly specified. This property provides some protection against misspecification of the full causal model. The features used were the same as for the Explainable S-Learner, with onset-to-treatment times grouped as early or late.
