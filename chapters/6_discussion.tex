\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}

In 74,678 patients with ischaemic stroke and an NIHSS score above 5 (or a recorded LVO) who arrived within six hours of onset, every reperfusion strategy was associated with more good outcomes at discharge, and the benefit depended strongly on when each treatment was given. Early combined treatment gave the largest benefit, followed by early MT alone and early IVT alone; early IVT followed by late MT gave no more than early IVT alone. Once IVT had been given, the additional benefit of MT was lost when MT took place more than about 4.5 to 6 hours after onset (later when IVT itself had been given later), whereas without IVT it persisted to 9.5--11 hours. Three methods with different assumptions agreed on this pattern. A fuller discussion is given in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp_discussion}.

\subsection{Time to IVT and time to MT together}

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe how the benefit of reperfusion varies with both onset-to-IVT and onset-to-MT times in a national population not selected by advanced imaging. Two patterns emerged. First, IVT added substantially to MT when given within about two hours of onset (an odds ratio of about 1.35--1.55 across MT times of 3--6 hours), less at 2--3 hours, and little when first given after three hours and followed promptly by MT. Second, once IVT had been given, MT added much less, and its added benefit was lost sooner. Within each patient, the additional benefit of MT fell to zero when MT took place about 4.6 hours after onset if IVT had been given within three hours, and about 5.7 hours if IVT had been given at three to 4.5 hours, compared with 9.5--11 hours without IVT (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:s_learner_mt_time_by_ivt}). Comparing patients given both treatments with different patients given IVT alone placed this point later, at six to seven hours. The earlier IVT was given, the more combined treatment gained over MT alone (Figure~\ref{fig:main_mt_time_by_ivt}), and the less was left for MT to add. The benefits of the two treatments were less than additive, as expected of treatments that reperfuse the same tissue.

These findings agree with the randomised trials that compared MT with and without IVT given first. Those trials enrolled patients who arrived directly at an MT centre, and the benefit of adding IVT fell the later it was given: it was no longer statistically significant beyond about 2 hours 20 minutes from onset, and the point estimate reached no benefit at about 3 hours \cite{kaesmacher2024}, the same gradient as we found across IVT within two hours, at two to three hours and at three to 4.5 hours. In the UK, most patients receive IVT at their local hospital and are then transferred for MT \cite{allen2022}, so MT comes later. For these patients, in whom MT follows IVT by hours, our results suggest that early IVT adds more benefit than it did in the trials, where the two were given close together: compared with MT alone at the same time, IVT within two hours added 0.27 log odds units when MT took place at 3 hours, rising to 0.42 at 6 hours.

\subsection{Comparison with randomised trials}

For IVT alone, early treatment (within 4.5 hours) increased good outcomes by 12--13 percentage points and late treatment by 6--9, and the benefit fell to zero at 6.8 hours. The alteplase meta-analysis, using an excellent outcome (mRS 0--1) at 3--6 months, found gains of 10 percentage points within 3 hours and 5 at 3--4.5 hours, falling to zero at 6.3 hours \cite{emberson_effect_2014}. For MT alone, each hour of delay multiplied the odds ratio for a good outcome by 0.86, similar to the common odds ratio of 0.84 per hour of delay to reperfusion in HERMES \cite{saver2016}. For early IVT plus early MT, 46.5\% of patients had a good outcome against 23.0\% of untreated controls. This is similar to the 46.0\% and 26.5\% functionally independent in the HERMES arms, although HERMES assessed outcome at 90 days and most of its controls received IVT \cite{goyal2016}. That the benefit of MT alone reached zero at about 9.5 hours does not conflict with the late-window trials, which selected patients by imaging \cite{nogueira2018,albers2018}; our estimate describes the average cost of delay in patients who arrive early.

\subsection{Agreement between methods}

Matching and the causal forest, which estimate the same quantity, agreed to within 2.2 percentage points for every strategy (for example, 23.5 and 23.7 percentage points for early combined treatment). Neither adjusted for onset-to-scan time, which largely determines whether early treatment is possible: adjusting for it compares early-treated patients with untreated patients scanned equally early, many of whom had a specific reason not to be treated. It may, however, also capture confounding, and when the causal forest additionally adjusted for it, the gains from strategies including early treatment were about 3--9 percentage points smaller (15.2 percentage points for early combined treatment), while those from late treatment changed by 1 percentage point or less. We therefore regard the primary estimates and those adjusted for onset-to-scan time as plausible upper and lower values for the benefit of early treatment.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}

The study used national data covering six years, from patients not selected by advanced imaging or trial criteria, with three causal methods within a target trial framework and openly available code. The principal limitation is that treatment was not randomised. The effects of IVT alone would survive unmeasured confounding as strong as any measured characteristic, but the MT effects could be overturned by a hidden factor as strong as stroke severity or pre-stroke disability, and LVO status and imaging findings, which we could not fully account for, could be this strong. Because nearly all patients given MT had an LVO, whereas many of their untreated controls probably did not, our estimates may understate the benefit of MT; conversely, untreated patients who arrived early may often have been unsuitable for treatment for reasons not recorded, which would overstate it. Treatment groups were defined by events after arrival, and outcome was assessed at discharge rather than at 90 days. Our findings complement rather than replace randomised trials. Further limitations are discussed in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp_discussion}.

\subsection{Implications}

The results support giving IVT as early as possible to patients who are also candidates for MT, particularly those who face a wait or a transfer before MT, and give no support to withholding early IVT in order to proceed to MT. IVT first given more than about three hours after onset adds little when MT follows promptly, in agreement with the trials \cite{kaesmacher2024,tnkplus2026}. For services, the added value of MT after IVT was largely lost when MT took place more than about 4.5 to 7 hours after onset, so shortening the interval between IVT and MT, by faster transfer or by bringing MT closer to patients, is likely to yield substantial gains. These estimates do not support withholding MT after IVT: we could not identify patients in whom IVT had failed to restore flow, who are those most likely to proceed to late MT, and imaging-selected patients benefit from MT up to 24 hours after onset \cite{nogueira2018,albers2018}.

\section{Conclusion}

In routine practice in England and Wales, IVT and MT are each effective in patients with ischaemic stroke and an NIHSS score above 5, or a recorded LVO, who reach hospital within six hours, with time dependence that closely matches the randomised trials. The greatest benefit comes from giving both treatments early, and stroke services should aim to minimise both times, not either one alone.
