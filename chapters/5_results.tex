Those receiving thrombolysis, with or without thrombectomy, had significantly lower diagnosis of atrial fibrillation, and use of anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation. Other characteristics were similar between groups.

Figure \ref{fig:main_target_trial_mrs_2} shows the differences between control and treated groups for each treatment group. Early (treatment given $\leq$ 270 minutes after stroke onset) combined thrombolysis and thrombectomy had the greatest estimated benefit, followed by early thrombolysis alone. Early thrombectomy alone had significant benefit, but less than combined treatment or thrombolysis alone. Late thrombolysis alone ($>$ 270 minutes after stroke onset) had reduced benefit, but late thrombectomy , with or without thrombolysis, appeared to have little benefit.

\subsection{S-Learner}

Figure \ref{fig:main_s_learner_benefit} shows the estimated treatment effect of each treatment group, when estimated by the S-Learner. Early (treatment given $\leq$ 270 minutes after stroke onset) combined thrombolysis and thrombectomy had the greatest estimated benefit, followed by early thrombolysis alone. Early thrombectomy alone had significant benefit, but less than combined treatment or thrombolysis alone. Late thrombolysis alone ($>$ 270 minutes after stroke onset) had reduced benefit. Late thrombectomy had reduced benefit again, with or without thrombolysis (though there was high variance on late thrombolysis and late thrombectomy, due to the small sample size).

Figure \ref{fig:main_s_learner_matrix} used the S-Learner fitted to continuous times to thrombolysis and thrombectomy, then grouped into one hour intervals. Both thrombolysis or thrombectomy alone show reducing benefit over the six hours after stroke onset. The magnitude of benefit is similar across each treatment for comparable times. Combined treatment shows some benefit over either treatment alone.

When a linear regression model is fitted to time to thrombolysis alone, the maximum log-odds benefit (in being discharged mRS 0-2) when extrapolated back to time of stroke onset was 1.27 (95\% c.i. 1.25 - 1.30). Time of no effect, after stroke onset, was 392 mins (95\% c.i. 382 - 402 minutes). For thrombectomy alone, the maximum log-odds benefit when extrapolated back to time of stroke onset was 1.45 (95\% c.i. 1.36 - 1.54). Time of no effect, after stroke onset, was 530 mins (95\% c.i. 470 - 589 minutes).

After estimating treatment effects we fitted an XGBoost regression model to understand what influences the benefit of treatment. This model predicted estimated benefit with an r-squared of 0.789. Figure \ref{fig:main_s_learner_benefit_shap_mrs_2} shows the SHAP values for that model, in order of overall effect of treatment benefit. The most influential features affecting the estimated benefit of treatment (in just the treated population of patients) were the stroke team attended, stroke severity, prior disability, and the type and time of treatment.

Early (treatment given $\leq$ 270 minutes after stroke onset) combined thrombolysis and thrombectomy had the greatest estimated benefit, followed by early thrombolysis alone. Early thrombectomy alone had significant benefit, but less than combined treatment or thrombolysis alone. Late thrombolysis alone ($>$ 270 minutes after stroke onset) had reduced benefit. Late thrombectomy had reduced benefit again, with or without thrombolysis (though there was high variance on late thrombolysis and late thrombectomy, due to the small sample size).
