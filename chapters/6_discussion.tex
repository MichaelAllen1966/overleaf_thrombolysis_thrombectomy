\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}

In 74,678 patients with ischaemic stroke and an NIHSS score above 5 (or a recorded LVO) who arrived within six hours of onset, every reperfusion strategy was associated with more good outcomes at discharge, and the benefit depended strongly on when each treatment was given. Early combined treatment gave the largest benefit, followed by early MT alone and early IVT alone; early IVT followed by late MT gave no more than early IVT alone. Three methods with different assumptions agreed on this pattern. A fuller discussion is given in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp_discussion}.

\subsection{Time to IVT and time to MT together}

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe how the benefit of reperfusion varies with both onset-to-IVT and onset-to-MT times in a national population not selected by advanced imaging. Two patterns emerged. First, IVT added substantially to MT when given within about two hours of onset (an odds ratio of about 1.35--1.55 across MT times of 3--6 hours), less at 2--3 hours, and little when first given after three hours and followed promptly by MT. Second, for a fixed time to IVT, the benefit of combined treatment converged on that of IVT alone as MT was delayed, so that most of the added value of MT over early IVT was lost by six to seven hours after onset. The benefits of the two treatments were less than additive, as expected of treatments that reperfuse the same tissue.

These findings agree with the randomised trials that compared MT with and without IVT given first. Those trials enrolled patients who arrived directly at an MT centre, and the benefit of adding IVT fell the later it was given, and was lost at about three hours after onset \cite{kaesmacher2024}. In the UK, most patients receive IVT at their local hospital and are then transferred for MT \cite{allen2022}, so MT comes later. For these patients, our results suggest that early IVT adds more benefit than it did in the trials.

\subsection{Comparison with randomised trials}

For IVT alone, early treatment (within 4.5 hours) increased good outcomes by 10--12 percentage points and late treatment by 6--10, and the benefit fell to zero at 6.8 hours. The alteplase meta-analysis found gains of 10 percentage points within 3 hours and 5 at 3--4.5 hours, falling to zero at 6.3 hours \cite{emberson_effect_2014}. For MT alone, the benefit fell with delay at a similar rate to HERMES: each hour multiplied the odds ratio by 0.86, against 0.84 \cite{saver2016}. For early IVT followed by MT, 46.5\% of patients had a good outcome against 23.0\% of untreated controls. This is similar to the 46.0\% and 26.5\% functionally independent in the HERMES arms, although most HERMES controls received IVT \cite{goyal2016}. That the benefit of MT alone reached zero at about 9.5 hours does not conflict with the late-window trials, which selected patients by imaging \cite{nogueira2018,albers2018}; our estimate describes the average cost of delay in patients who arrive early.

\subsection{Agreement between methods}

The causal forest gave smaller gains than matching for early treatment (15.2 against 23.5 percentage points for early combined treatment), almost entirely because it adjusted for onset-to-scan time. This may remove confounding, but it also compares early-treated patients with untreated patients scanned equally early, many of whom had a specific reason not to be treated. We therefore regard the two estimates as plausible upper and lower values.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}

The study used national data covering six years, from patients not selected by advanced imaging or trial criteria, with three causal methods within a target trial framework and openly available code. The principal limitation is that treatment was not randomised. The effects of IVT alone would survive unmeasured confounding as strong as any measured characteristic, but the MT effects could be overturned by a hidden factor as strong as stroke severity or pre-stroke disability, and LVO status and imaging findings, which we could not fully account for, could be this strong. Confirmed LVO in every patient given MT would bias towards underestimating its benefit, and unsuitability of untreated early arrivals towards overestimating it. Treatment groups were defined by events after arrival, and outcome was assessed at discharge rather than at 90 days. Our findings complement rather than replace randomised trials. Further limitations are discussed in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:supp_discussion}.

\subsection{Implications}

The results support giving IVT as early as possible to patients who are also candidates for MT, particularly those who face a wait or a transfer before MT, and give no support to withholding early IVT in order to proceed to MT. IVT first given more than about three hours after onset adds little when MT follows promptly, in agreement with the trials \cite{kaesmacher2024,tnkplus2026}. For services, most of the added value of MT over early IVT was lost when MT was delayed beyond about six hours, so shortening the interval between IVT and MT, by faster transfer or by bringing MT closer to patients, is likely to yield substantial gains.

\section{Conclusion}

In routine practice in England and Wales, IVT and MT are each effective in patients with ischaemic stroke and an NIHSS score above 5, or identified as having a LVO stroke, who reach hospital within six hours, with time dependence that closely matches the randomised trials. The greatest benefit comes from giving both treatments early, and stroke services should aim to minimise both times, not either one alone.
