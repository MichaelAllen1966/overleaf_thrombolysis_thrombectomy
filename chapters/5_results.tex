\section{Results}

\subsection{Patients}

Patients who received MT, with or without IVT, had more severe strokes and less pre-stroke disability than those who received IVT alone, and those who received MT alone were far more likely to have AF and to be taking anticoagulants (Table~\ref{tab:matching}). Matching produced closely balanced groups, and at most 2.6\% of any group could not be matched, although diabetes was less common in patients given MT than in their matched controls.

\subsection{Effect of each treatment strategy}

Every treatment strategy was associated with a higher chance of a good outcome (Table~\ref{tab:effects}). All three methods found that early IVT plus early MT gave the largest benefit: 46.5\% of treated patients had a good outcome against 23.0\% of matched controls, the S-learner odds ratio was 3.2 (log odds ratio 1.16), and the causal forest estimated a gain of 15.2 percentage points. Early MT alone gave the next largest benefit, followed by early IVT alone. The causal forest gave smaller gains than matching for the three early strategies, but similar gains for the late strategies. Late treatment gave about half to two thirds of the benefit of early treatment on the log odds scale, and early IVT followed by late MT gave no more than early IVT alone. Only 141 patients received both treatments late, and their estimates were imprecise.

\subsection{Effect of time to treatment}

Figure~\ref{fig:main_s_learner_matrix} shows the S-learner's estimated benefit by time to IVT and time to MT. The benefit of each treatment given alone declined steadily with delay, reaching zero at 406 minutes (95\% CI 402--409) for IVT and 570 minutes (563--577) for MT; for MT, each hour of delay multiplied the odds ratio for a good outcome by about 0.86. The two treatment times interacted. For a fixed time to MT, benefit was greater the earlier IVT had been given: with MT at 240--300 minutes, the log odds ratio rose from 0.68 without IVT to 1.06 with IVT at 60--120 minutes. For a fixed time to IVT, benefit fell the later MT followed: with IVT at 60--120 minutes, it fell from 1.71 with MT at 60--120 minutes to 0.81 with MT at 360--420 minutes, close to the 0.84 for IVT alone. The benefit of early combined treatment (1.16) was less than the sum of those of early IVT alone and early MT alone (0.64 and 1.00).

\subsection{Characteristics associated with greater benefit}

The features most associated with the size of benefit were stroke severity, the type and timing of treatment, the stroke team attended, age and pre-stroke disability (Figure~\ref{fig:main_s_learner_benefit_shap_mrs_2}). Benefit increased with stroke severity up to an NIHSS score of about 20, and was smaller with greater pre-stroke disability and in patients aged 85 or over.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}

In every sensitivity analysis, each strategy remained associated with more good outcomes, mostly with changes of 3 percentage points or less (Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Adding comorbidities to the matching reduced the estimate for early MT alone from 17.2 to 12.6 percentage points, and adjusting for LVO, where recorded, increased the MT estimates. Without adjustment for onset-to-scan time, the causal forest estimates for early treatment came within 1.5 percentage points of matching. The effects of IVT alone would survive a hidden confounder three times as strong as stroke severity, age or onset-to-scan time, but one as strong as stroke severity or pre-stroke disability could remove or halve the benefit of MT.
