\section{Patients and methods}

\subsection{Data and patients}

We used anonymised data from the Sentinel Stroke National Audit Programme (SSNAP), the national stroke registry for England and Wales, for admissions in 2020--2025 (Healthcare Quality Improvement Partnership data request HQIP515). We included patients with ischaemic stroke who arrived within 6 hours of known onset, had an NIHSS score above 5 or a recorded LVO. We excluded patients without a recorded discharge modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, with recorded use of perfusion imaging, who received IVT 6 hours or more or MT 12 hours or more after onset, or who were recorded as receiving IVT after MT. Of the 74,678 patients included, 28,884 received IVT alone, 2,983 MT alone, 5,164 both, and 37,647 neither (patient flow diagrams are given in Supplementary Figures~\ref{fig:flow_tte}--\ref{fig:flow_cf}).

\subsection{Treatment groups and outcome}

Patients were grouped by the reperfusion treatment they received (IVT alone, MT alone, or both) and its timing, with each treatment classed as early ($\leq$270 minutes from onset) or late ($>$270 minutes). A good outcome was a discharge mRS of 0--2, or no worse than before the stroke, so that patients with pre-stroke disability who returned to their previous level of function had a good outcome. All three methods estimated the effect of treatment in the patients who received it (the average treatment effect in the treated, ATT), compared with no reperfusion treatment.

\subsection{Causal inference methods}

We used three causal inference methods resting on different assumptions; full descriptions and a study protocol for each method are given in Supplementary Sections~\ref{sec:supp_methods} and~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\textit{Target trial emulation by matching.} The analysis was designed to mirror the randomised trial that would ideally have been run \cite{hernan2016}. For each treatment group, every treated patient was matched to an untreated patient with the same pre-stroke mRS, age band, NIHSS band (0--4, 5--9, 10--14, \textit{et seq.}), atrial fibrillation (AF) diagnosis and anticoagulant use for AF \cite{stuart2010matching}. Each untreated patient could be used as a control up to three times. At most 2.6\% of treated patients in any group could not be matched. The proportion with a good outcome was compared between treated patients and their matched controls.

\textit{S-learner.} A single machine learning model (XGBoost \cite{chen_xgboost_2016}) predicted each patient's chance of a good outcome from their characteristics, stroke team and the treatment received, with treatment represented either by the actual onset-to-treatment times or by the early/late groups. Each treated patient's benefit was the difference between their predicted outcome with the treatment received and with that treatment removed \cite{kunzel2019metalearners}. Predictions came from models that had not seen the patient (five-fold cross-validation). A second model, explained with SHAP values \cite{shap_documentation}, identified the characteristics associated with greater benefit.

\textit{Causal forest.} A causal forest within a double machine learning framework \cite{chernozhukov2018double,wager2018estimation} first predicted each patient's outcome and their likely treatment from their characteristics, stroke team, year of admission and onset-to-scan time, and then estimated treatment effects from the variation that these predictions could not explain. Effects in the treated were estimated by augmented inverse-probability weighting. This approach is doubly robust: it remains reliable if either the outcome or the treatment model is correct. The 67 patients with missing data were excluded.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}

For each method, we repeated the analysis changing one choice at a time: the early/late cut-off, the definition of a good outcome, the characteristics used for matching or adjustment (including comorbidities, LVO, stroke team and onset-to-scan time) and model settings. We assessed unmeasured confounding with E-values, the minimum strength of association that a hidden confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to explain away an effect \cite{vanderweele2017evalue}, and with the sensemakr method of Cinelli and Hazlett, which compares such a confounder with measured characteristics such as stroke severity \cite{cinelli2020sensemakr}.
